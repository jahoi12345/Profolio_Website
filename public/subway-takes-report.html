What's Kareem's Take?

A full statistical read of 787 "Subway Takes" clips: every rider's gender, Kareem's verdict, the topic, and how it all moved with views.

Author

James Li

Published

September 17, 2026

Coverage

787 clips · Aug 2023 – Sep 2026

Source

@SubwayTakes, YouTube Shorts

Abstract

This report analyzes all 787 short-form clips from the YouTube channel @SubwayTakes, in which host Kareem Rahma asks New York (and London) subway riders for a "take," then agrees, disagrees, or reverses course. Every clip's transcript was read manually to classify Kareem's verdict, topic, and confidence; rider gender was hand-labeled separately via a keyboard-driven tool. Women's takes are agreed with more often than men's (66.0% vs. 58.7%, strengthening to p = 0.007 on the highest-confidence subset). Verdict matters more for engagement than who gives the take: clips where Kareem reverses position mid-conversation out-view a plain agreement by roughly 54%, while gender itself has no measurable effect on views. Re-cutting and re-posting the same conversation does not reliably help once channel growth is controlled for: original uploads outperform their own later re-cuts on a cohort-relative basis (p = 0.002). Clip duration is the strongest single predictor of views; profanity has none. A follow-up section audits the classification itself via a blind self-consistency check (80.6% agreement on re-read, rising to 100% on high-confidence rows) and an unsupervised topic-modeling comparison.

1. Kareem agrees with women more often

Verdict by gender

Excluding the 52 clips with no clear verdict, women's takes land an "agree" 66.0% of the time versus 58.7% for men; women also get flipped-into-a-reversal ("mixed") less often.

The gap is a suggestive 7.3-point edge for women, but it sits right at the edge of conventional significance: a two-way agree-vs-not chi-square gives p = 0.052; the full three-way split (agree/disagree/mixed) gives p = 0.118. Read it as a real but modest lean, not a settled effect: the show simply hasn't run enough episodes yet to be sure.

Men's takes are almost 1.3× more likely to end in a "mixed" verdict (12.4% vs 9.4%); Kareem reverses his position on a man's take somewhat more often than a woman's.

χ² = 3.78, p = 0.052 (agree vs. not) · χ² = 4.27, p = 0.118 (3-way)

2. Food is the runaway favorite subject

What riders take on

57 of 787 takes are about food, more than double the next category. Relationships, music, technology and dating round out the top five.

3. Some subjects are agreement magnets, others are landmines

Verdict & gender by topic

Fashion (84%), friendship (88%) and culture (75%) takes get agreed with far more than average. Media, mostly the vertical-video/phone-format debates, is the most contested subject, disagreed with 61% of the time. It's also the most male-skewed topic in the whole corpus.

4. Views took off in mid-2024, and stayed up

The show over time

Average views per clip jumped roughly 30–50× between the show's first two quarters (2023 Q3–Q4, ~30–47K average views) and mid-2024 onward (consistently 800K–1.6M). Kareem's agree-rate has stayed in a fairly narrow 48–68% band the entire run, with no clear long-term drift.

5. Early clips were rougher, and it shows in the data

Data quality over time

The show's first two quarters account for a disproportionate share of "unclear" verdicts and re-used/fragment footage; production got tighter as the format matured.

In 2023 Q3, 35.7% of clips had no resolvable verdict (teaser cuts, monologue fragments, duplicated footage). By 2025, that rate is at or near 0% in most quarters. Of the corpus overall, 26 clips are exact or near-duplicate re-cuts of another clip, and 24 are teaser/fragment cuts with no second-party response, both concentrated in the early run.

6. A Kareem reversal outperforms a plain agreement

What drives views

Median views by verdict: clips where Kareem flips his position mid-conversation ("mixed") out-view a clean "agree" by roughly 54%. Gender, on the other hand, barely moves the needle.

Median views: mixed 740,908 > disagree 640,904 > agree 480,916 > unclear 53,881. The pattern holds for likes and comments too: the drama of a change of heart is the most rewatchable outcome on the show, and an unresolved clip is by far the least.

By gender, median views are close (male 542,885 vs. female 480,916); a Welch t-test on log-views gives p = 0.33; no real difference. Popularity is driven by what happens in the conversation, not who's giving the take.

ARI ≈ 0.03 is barely above chance: overall, word-level clustering does not reproduce my topic buckets. But it isn't uniformly bad: clusters built almost entirely from distinctive vocabulary (Taylor Swift, pets, pizza, race, vertical video) line up with my hand labels at 70–100% purity. Broad, discourse-driven buckets like "relationships" or "philosophy" don't have a distinctive vocabulary to cluster on, so an unsupervised method can't confirm or refute those calls; only a human read can.

Read: concrete/lexical topics are machine-checkable; abstract/conversational topics are not.

9. Any named guest roughly doubles the view count, but fame level doesn't matter further

Does a cameo help?

111 clips credit a named guest in the title ("Feat."/"Ft."). 37 of those are recognizable public figures (Bill Burr, Matt Damon, Charlize Theron, Riz Ahmed, Halsey…); the other 74 are named but not A-list.

Median views: no cameo 475,602 → named guest 786,595 → celebrity 993,627. Both cameo tiers beat "no cameo" with high confidence (celebrity p = 0.001, named-guest p < 0.0001, Welch t-test on log-views), but celebrity vs. named-guest are statistically indistinguishable from each other. Having any credited second participant is what correlates with reach; A-list fame on top of that doesn't measurably add more.

10. Raw views say yes. Controlling for channel growth says no.

Do re-cuts of the same clip do better?

22 duplicate clusters (30 re-cut pairs): the same underlying conversation, re-posted under a new title, sometimes a year-plus later. Naively, re-cuts look great: several posted after the channel exploded in size pull in 20–200× the views of the original. But that's the channel getting bigger, not the re-cut being better.

Raw comparison: re-cuts "beat" the original 47% of the time; the naive median change is −18%, but the mean is +1,130%, entirely driven by a handful of re-cuts posted long after the channel's audience had multiplied.

Normalized comparison (each clip's views ÷ the average views of everything posted that same quarter, i.e. performance relative to its own cohort): re-cuts beat their original on this basis only 23% of the time. Paired t-test on log-normalized views: p = 0.002. Wilcoxon: p = 0.005. Once you strip out audience growth, first-posted cuts reliably outperform their own later re-cuts.

Dots below the diagonal = the re-cut underperformed its cohort-relative baseline (23 of 30 do).

11. London out-performs, launched a year in, and skews more male

NYC vs. London ("Tube Takes")

52 clips (of 787) are the London spin-off, running since July 2024, about a year after the NYC show started.

Median views: London 796,894 vs. NYC 496,100: London clips out-view NYC clips by ~60%, a highly significant gap (p < 0.0001) even though it's the newer, smaller sub-format. London riders skew more male (69.2% vs. 58.9% in NYC), though with only 52 London clips that gender gap itself isn't statistically solid (χ² p = 0.19); worth revisiting once the format has more episodes.

13. Yes, a real one, but it's not about holiday-themed content

Is there a holiday bump?

Each clip's views, divided by the average for everything posted that same quarter (removing the channel's overall growth trend), grouped by calendar month across all years.

November and December post a median cohort-relative performance of 0.85–0.91 vs. 0.44 for the rest of the year, roughly double and highly significant (p < 0.0001, n = 137 holiday-month clips vs. 650 others). That's a genuine, reproducible seasonal effect, not a fluke of channel growth.

But it isn't driven by holiday-themed content: the Sarah Jessica-Parker-as-tree-topper clip performs almost exactly average for its month, and the "mashed potatoes" clip that mentions Thanksgiving wasn't even posted in Nov/Dec: it went up in October and underperforms badly (0.15× its cohort). The bump looks like a general year-end viewing/algorithm effect, not audiences specifically rewarding holiday subject matter.

14. A wavering disagreement performs just like a "mixed" verdict

Not all "disagree"s are equal

175 of the 199 "disagree" clips hold a firm line throughout; 24 show real wavering: hedging language in the transcript itself (concessions, visible hesitation) despite the final verdict staying "disagree."

Median views: agree 480,916 · firm disagree 601,529 · wavering disagree 1,136,163 · mixed 740,908. Wavering disagreement isn't just directionally higher than firm disagreement (not significant at this sample size, n=24, p = 0.21); it's statistically indistinguishable from "mixed" (p = 0.99). The category label matters less than whether real tension shows up in the conversation.

Methodology: 787 of 788 shorts from the @SubwayTakes channel (one duplicate-titled fetch resolved out). Rider gender was hand-labeled clip-by-clip by the analyst via a keyboard-driven labeling tool, not inferred from thumbnails or voice. Agreement, topic and confidence were read directly off each clip's transcript (native captions where available, local Whisper transcription otherwise), no vision model or third-party classifier API was used. Topic labels were written per-clip during classification and grouped here into topic_broad buckets for aggregation. Full dataset: data/subwaytakes_dataset.csv.
